# GitHub Actions Leads; Jenkins, Gitea Defend Niches

As of November 2026, GitHub Actions is the sensible default for teams already on GitHub, Jenkins is the tool to keep where its flexibility or isolation is actually in use, and Gitea Actions fits small and mid-sized private instances but is not yet ready for regulated delivery. The real choice is who owns the control plane. GitHub Actions removes controller operations but adds exposure to GitHub's outages and to a fragile action supply chain. Jenkins gives the most control and SCM independence, but it demands monthly upgrades, constant plugin care, and Groovy expertise. Gitea Actions gives you GitHub-like YAML on a server you own, but released versions ignore `environment` deployment gates and lack OIDC, and its 2026 security record includes an actively exploited RCE. Survey data shows the momentum: GitHub Actions rose to 47% of organizations while Jenkins slipped to 28%, and 91% of cloud-native respondents would recommend Actions against 66% for Jenkins. Two limits apply to everything below: no survey isolates senior engineers' criteria, and no independent total-cost study compares these tools, so the senior perspective here is inferred from switching reasons, case studies, and practitioner critiques. Self-hosted runners do not change this picture much, because they move compute but leave GitHub's control plane in the path.

## GitHub Actions leads at 47%, while Jenkins is trusted more than recommended

**JetBrains' 2026 survey puts GitHub Actions at 47% of organizations (41% in 2025), GitLab CI at 32% (34%), and Jenkins at 28% (31%)** ([JetBrains](https://www.jetbrains.com/pages/state-of-cicd2026/)). The sample is 598 respondents, the question is multi-select, and JetBrains sells TeamCity. A second JetBrains survey has Jenkins at 37%, and the CNCF's 2024 cloud-native survey shows GitHub Actions 51%, Jenkins 44%, and GitLab 43% ([CNCF](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/hubfs/Research%20Reports/cncf_annual_survey24_031225a.pdf?hsLang=en)). The defensible reading is that Actions is first, while Jenkins and GitLab CI trade second place depending on the sample. CNCF's rise for Jenkins from 32% to 44% coincided with a sample drop from 988 to 689, so it does not show a revival.

The more telling number is sentiment. In the CNCF's Q1 2026 Technology Radar, **Jenkins earned the highest 5-star maturity share in its category (47%) but only 66% would recommend it, against 91% for GitHub Actions** ([CNCF Radar](https://www.cncf.io/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Q1-2026-CNCF-Technology-Radar-Report.pdf)). That pattern reads as "trusted but not chosen again". It is one perception survey, so it supports the interpretation rather than proving it.

Two findings complicate a simple story of cloud winning. About 73% of respondents self-host their primary CI/CD tool, with 30.5% on their own premises, a five-year high, and 24.4% on a fully managed service ([JetBrains](https://www.jetbrains.com/pages/state-of-cicd2026/)). And 62% have no plans to switch tools; those who do cite scaling costs (24%), maintenance (23%), consolidation (22%), and complex configuration (21%) (same source). Those four reasons are the closest available proxy for senior decision criteria. **Gitea Actions appears in none of the survey data found**, so its adoption is unmeasured rather than shown to be small.

The table below condenses the comparison. It reflects this report's judgment, and the evidence for each row appears in the following sections.

| Criterion | Jenkins | GitHub Actions | Gitea Actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control plane | You run it | GitHub runs it; you cannot isolate from it | You run it |
| Main operating cost | Engineers: upgrades, plugins, Groovy | Minutes, plus time spent hardening workflows | Engineers: patching, runner upgrades, images |
| Deployment gates | Not researched here | Native environments and approvals | `environment` ignored |
| Keyless cloud auth | Not researched here | Mature per-job OIDC | Merged for unreleased 29.0.0 |
| Autoscaling | Kubernetes or image-based agents | Hosted runners or ARC | Enterprise only, or DIY |
| Biggest risk | Controller compromise through core and plugin flaws | Outages and action supply chain | Fast-moving code, exploited CVEs, unproven at scale |
| Best fit | Complex, SCM-agnostic, or isolated estates | Teams already on GitHub | Small and mid-sized private instances |

## GitHub Actions trades controller toil for control-plane and supply-chain risk

**The advantage is integration with almost no controller to run.** Enterprise accounts get 500 concurrent standard jobs and 1,000 on larger runners, on hardware from 1 to 96 cores plus GPU ([limits](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/limits), [pricing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/reference/actions-runner-pricing)). Per-job OIDC removes long-lived credentials for AWS, Azure, GCP, and Vault ([OIDC docs](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/concepts/security/openid-connect)). Standard hosted runners are free in public repos, and self-hosted usage is currently free ([billing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/concepts/product-billing/github-actions)). Hosted prices fell by up to 39% on January 1, 2026, and a Linux 2-core x64 minute costs $0.006. GitHub also postponed its planned $0.002 per minute self-hosted charge, saying it would "re-evaluate", which is not a cancellation ([GitHub Changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-12-16-coming-soon-simpler-pricing-and-a-better-experience-for-github-actions/)). Budgets should still model that fee returning. GitHub Agentic Workflows reached GA on October 28, 2026, compiling Markdown into standard Actions workflows ([GitHub Changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-10-28-github-agentic-workflows-generally-available/)), though no real-world quality data exists yet.

**Reliability is the weakest point in 2026, and self-hosting does not fix it.** One third-party tally counted 57 Actions outages (16 major) from May 2025 to April 2026, the most of any GitHub service ([IncidentHub](https://blog.incidenthub.cloud/github-reliability-outage-history-2025-2026)). Availability figures are disputed: an unofficial tracker showed 78.33% for April against roughly 99.9% on the official status page ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2026/06/12/github-outages-persist-as-ai-coding-drives-traffic-surge/5255125)). GitHub's own reports are the firmer evidence. On July 19–20 an expired certificate delayed 78.99% of larger-runner jobs, 29.8% of scale-set jobs, and 8.7% of self-hosted jobs by over five minutes ([July report](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/github-availability-report-july-2026/)). In August GitHub conceded that "shared infrastructure services have not kept up" with Actions growth, and described its routing fix as buying about three months of headroom ([August report](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/github-availability-report-august-2026/)). As of October 7, **only 13 of 51 primary MySQL databases had moved to Azure** ([September report](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/github-availability-report-september-2026/)). Migration risk therefore looks set to continue well into 2027, which is this report's inference rather than GitHub's statement.

**Supply-chain risk is the second structural weakness, though GitHub's response has improved quickly.** The tj-actions compromise in March 2025 reached CISA's KEV list ([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2025/03/18/supply-chain-compromise-third-party-github-action-cve-2025-30066)), although Palo Alto found only 218 repositories actually leaked secrets ([SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/impact-root-cause-of-github-actions-supply-chain-hack-revealed/)). In March 2026 attackers force-pushed 76 of 77 trivy-action tags, and even a safe SHA of trivy-action from before April 2025 could pull malicious code through an unpinned nested action ([Aqua](https://github.com/aquasecurity/trivy/security/advisories/GHSA-69fq-xp46-6x23)). That detail undercuts the common advice that SHA pinning alone is enough. Workflow lockfiles, which pin transitive dependencies and can be required by policy, reached GA on October 28, 2026 ([GitHub Changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-10-28-github-actions-workflow-lockfiles-are-generally-available/)); GHES availability is unconfirmed. **On December 2, 2026, a default policy starts blocking `pull_request_target` in public repos** that have no other applicable event policy ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/security/securely-using-pull_request_target)), so affected teams have a month to act. The native egress firewall was still a logging-only technical preview at last report ([GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/disrupting-supply-chain-attacks-on-npm-and-github-actions/)).

**The daily complaints concern developer experience.** Ian Duncan's critique cites a log viewer that struggles with large logs, odd expression semantics whose errors surface only after runner spin-up, opaque caching, and coarse concurrency ([Ian Duncan](https://www.iankduncan.com/engineering/2026-02-05-github-actions-killing-your-team/)). It is opinion from someone who recommends Buildkite for larger teams, but [Hacker News discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=43419701) echoes it. `act` does not reproduce all behavior locally ([OneUptime](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-02-08-how-to-run-act-to-test-github-actions-locally-in-docker/view)). A market of third-party runners (RunsOn, Depot, Blacksmith) addresses speed and unit cost, but all still depend on GitHub's YAML and job assignment. The Zig project's departure to Codeberg shows the reputational cost, with Actions bugs and unpredictable scheduling among its stated reasons ([heise](https://www.heise.de/en/news/Zig-turns-its-back-on-GitHub-Frustration-over-Actions-and-Microsoft-s-AI-course-11100039.html?view=print)).

## Jenkins gives maximum control at the price of a permanent patching job

**The strengths are independence and programmability.** Jenkins has no per-minute fee and is not tied to one SCM: its September 2026 advisories alone cover plugins for GitLab, Bitbucket, and Gitee ([Advisory](https://www.jenkins.io/security/advisory/2026-09-02/)). Its CPS engine lets Pipelines save state and resume after a controller restart ([Jenkins docs](https://www.jenkins.io/doc/book/pipeline/cps-method-mismatches/)), and agents can run on pre-baked images, containers, or virtual machines ([Jenkins docs](https://www.jenkins.io/doc/book/scaling/hardware-recommendations/)). Its reputation as the default for firewalled and regulated on-prem sites rests mostly on a competitor's writing ([Northflank](https://northflank.com/blog/github-actions-vs-jenkins)), and no first-hand 2025–2026 case studies for embedded or hardware-in-the-loop use turned up. The claim is plausible but thinly evidenced. Nobody should read "air-gapped" as "effort-free" either, since plugin and update-site mirrors become your own patching surface.

**The first cost is cadence.** The current LTS is 2.580.2 (October 28, 2026), and **LTS releases came every 28 days in 2026, with 6 of 11 carrying security fixes** ([LTS changelog](https://www.jenkins.io/changelog-stable/)). Java 17 is gone, and Java 21 support ends in September 2027, so a move to Java 25 belongs on 2027 plans ([Java policy](https://www.jenkins.io/doc/book/platform-information/support-policy-java/)). The project's rule of thumb is roughly 500 jobs per controller, which implies about 8 controllers for 1,200 developers, and each controller carries its own plugin updates, backups, and security setup ([Jenkins docs](https://www.jenkins.io/doc/book/scaling/architecting-for-scale/)). That page is old and undated, so treat it as a rough guide. Active/active HA is documented as a [CloudBees](https://docs.cloudbees.com/docs/cloudbees-ci/latest/ha/ha-fundamentals) feature, with limits on some Pipeline idioms and some upgrades that cannot be rolling. No public CloudBees pricing was found.

**The second cost is security exposure, which is unusually acute right now.** Jenkins published 33 advisories across 2024–2026, a steady pace, but **three advisories in the eight weeks to October 28, 2026 carried 68 CVEs** ([September 2](https://www.jenkins.io/security/advisory/2026-09-02/), [September 16](https://www.jenkins.io/security/advisory/2026-09-16/), [October 28](https://www.jenkins.io/security/advisory/2026-10-28/)). These include six High-severity Script Security sandbox bypasses, a setup-wizard race allowing unauthenticated admin creation, and flaws letting agent-supplied data attack the controller. The Parameterized Remote Trigger plugin stores tokens unencrypted and had no fix at publication, which means operators sometimes must remove a plugin rather than patch it. CVE-2024-23897 remains the cautionary case, reaching KEV with known ransomware use ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2024/08/cisa-warns-of-critical-jenkins.html)). The operating lesson is to treat the Groovy sandbox as a weak boundary, restrict who can change pipeline code, and keep untrusted PR builds well away from the controller. The cause of the autumn surge is unexplained, and it shows active fixing as well as active risk. The worst position is a lagging controller.

**Pipeline code carries its own burden.** CPS restrictions mean `@NonCPS` methods cannot call steps, and passing a CPS closure to a library sort can silently return wrong results ([Jenkins docs](https://www.jenkins.io/doc/book/pipeline/cps-method-mismatches/)). FreeAgent's reasons for leaving read like a checklist of senior complaints: a single controller halting all builds, poisoned agents, hard-to-test upgrades, unreviewed settings, and Groovy outside team expertise ([FreeAgent](http://engineering.freeagent.com/2022/06/10/in-full-flow-moving-from-jenkins-to-actions-part-1/)). Slack, citing security issues, downtime, and poor UX, moved a majority of its CI jobs to Actions ([Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/migration-automation-easing-the-jenkins-%E2%86%92-gha-shift-with-help-from-ai/)).

## Gitea Actions copies the syntax but still lacks gates, OIDC, and proof at scale

**Gitea's pitch is familiar YAML on infrastructure you own, and 2026 closed real gaps.** Current stable is 28.3.0 (November 1, 2026), which pairs with Gitea Runner 5.x, a hard fork of `nektos/act` ([Gitea docs](https://docs.gitea.com/usage/actions/overview), [28.3.0](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-28.3.0/)). Concurrency arrived in 1.26, first-class reusable workflows in 1.27, and dynamic matrices in 28.0 ([1.26](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-1.26.0/), [1.27](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-1.27.0/), [28.0](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-28.0.0/)). Stock artifact v4 and cache actions now work without the old fork ([Runner 3.0](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-runner-3.0.0/)). Anything written about `act_runner` or Gitea 1.2x before mid-2026 is therefore likely stale. Low-quality sources with no stated method claim about 200 MB of server memory ([Luca Berton](https://lucaberton.com/blog/gitea-self-hosted-git-server-kubernetes-2026/)), and a low-authority forum thread describes a small team running it in production for over a year ([forum thread](https://yomotherboard.com/question/has-anyone-tried-running-gitea-actions-for-production-workloads/)).

**The gaps fall exactly where serious delivery needs controls.** **`jobs.<job_id>.environment` is ignored**, so GitHub-style deployment approvals do not exist, and `id-token` is unsupported in 28.x ([Gitea docs](https://docs.gitea.com/usage/actions/comparison)). OIDC merged on October 18 only for the unreleased 29.0.0 ([PR #39052](https://github.com/go-gitea/gitea/pull/39052)). `GITEA_TOKEN` cannot push to its own repo's package registry, and PR runs check out the head ref rather than a merge preview ([FAQ](https://docs.gitea.com/usage/actions/faq/)), so CI tests a different thing than GitHub's merge ref does. External reusable workflows by URL are no longer supported. Autoscaling Kubernetes runners, audit logs, and SAML SSO are Enterprise features ([pricing](https://about.gitea.com/pricing/)), and the free job-pod mode cannot run Docker actions, while the DinD alternative is privileged and escapable ([Kubernetes docs](https://docs.gitea.com/runner/installation/kubernetes/)). Compatibility is real at the YAML level but not across the marketplace, and no source measures how many actions fail.

**Security and churn are the operational price.** **CVE-2026-60004 let ordinary repo write access run shell commands as the Gitea OS user, with open registration exposing it to anyone who could sign up; CISA listed it in KEV on August 25** ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/08/26/gitea-cve-2026-60004-exploited-in-the-wild/)). Actions-specific flaws recur as a class: fork-PR approval bypasses and over-broad task tokens appear in 1.27.3, 28.0.0, and 28.3.0 ([1.27.3](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-1.27.3/), [28.0](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-28.0.0/), [28.3](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-28.3.0/)). The 1.27.0 release post alone lists about 45 CVEs by this research's count. No comparable earlier-year count or independent audit exists, so 2026 volume cannot be separated from increased scrutiny. The runner went through three major versions in about ten weeks, with six breaking changes in 5.0 ([Runner 5.0](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-runner-5.0.0/)), and 28.0 began deleting completed runs, logs, and artifacts after 400 days by default ([28.0](https://blog.gitea.com/release-of-28.0.0/)). Runner upgrades deserve staging tests rather than background patching. A default install also resolves unqualified actions from github.com, so air-gapped sites must mirror them and set `DEFAULT_ACTIONS_URL = self` ([Gitea docs](https://docs.gitea.com/usage/actions/comparison)).

**Evidence of enterprise use is absent, and sovereignty evidence points elsewhere.** The only named user found is Gitea itself, and rival Forgejo notes Gitea's core is developed on GitHub using GitHub Actions ([Forgejo](https://forgejo.org/compare-to-gitea/)). The strongest institutional sovereignty move found, the Dutch government's code.overheid.nl, chose Forgejo, per secondary press ([byteiota](https://byteiota.com/netherlands-builds-github-rival-forgejo-migration-2026/)). Teams choosing self-hosted forges for governance reasons should evaluate both. Forgejo offers non-profit stewardship, a dated LTS schedule, and OIDC today, but it aims for "familiarity" rather than compatibility and ignores job `permissions` ([Forgejo docs](https://forgejo.org/docs/latest/user/actions/github-actions/), [v17](https://forgejo.org/2026-10-release-v17-0/)). Gitea looks stronger for Actions portability and Kubernetes job pods, and the two should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Migrations pay through rewrites, and engineer time decides cost

**Credible case studies show gains, but none isolates the platform's effect.** Bankdata cut a pipeline's mean runtime by 82.1%, from 724 to 130 seconds, moving from Jenkins to Actions. The authors call this an upper bound because it mixes architecture and platform effects, and the team lost step-level visibility ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00936v1)). FreeAgent cut monthly test-infrastructure spend by 90%, but it did so with ephemeral self-managed EC2 fleets ([FreeAgent](http://engineering.freeagent.com/2022/06/10/in-full-flow-moving-from-jenkins-to-actions-part-1/)). "Actions made it faster" is better read as "the rewrite made it faster". That matters because a team could capture part of the gain by modernizing in place, although Bankdata's controlled test on Jenkins was blocked and no one has measured this directly.

**Plan for automation to handle about half the work at best.** At Slack, whose migration tool covered 242 pipelines across 3 Jenkins instances, GitHub Actions Importer fully converted about 50% of pipelines and failed on about 5%. Eight unsupported steps and environments caused over 90% of full-conversion failures. Slack's projected saving of 1,300+ hours is a projection, and developers later reported errors in generated workflows ([Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/migration-automation-easing-the-jenkins-%E2%86%92-gha-shift-with-help-from-ai/)). The Importer does not support `matrix` or `parameters`, and it leaves scripted pipelines, secrets, runners, and unknown plugins as manual tasks ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/tutorials/migrate-to-github-actions/automated-migrations/jenkins-migration)). Those are precisely the features of mature estates, so its `audit` command works better as a scoping tool than a conversion engine. **No first-party source publishes a person-month or dollar cost for a large Jenkins migration**, and no Jenkins-to-Gitea case study with metrics turned up.

**Total cost remains unmeasured, but the arithmetic is instructive.** At list price and before allowances, 35,200 Linux 2-core minutes per month ([The Stack's example](https://www.thestack.technology/github-to-charge-self-hosted-actions-users-what-does-that-look-like/)) costs $211 hosted, and 1.6M minutes costs $9,600. A postponed $0.002 fee would add $70 or $3,200 on self-hosted equivalents. Set against a vendor-quoted $150,000–$200,000 annual engineer cost ([HAMS Tech](https://www.hams.tech/blog/jenkins-modernization-myth-groovy-jcasc-2026.html)), roughly $12,500–$16,700 per month, even 1.6M hosted minutes costs less than one engineer. That comparison is rough: it omits your own compute, storage, and larger runners, and a 16-core minute costs seven times as much. Public Jenkins staffing estimates are nearly useless, spanning [5–10 hours per month](https://cicdcost.com/jenkins-self-hosted-cost) to [2–5 FTEs](https://www.harness.io/comparison-guide/harness-ci-vs-jenkins), all from search snippets and mostly from parties with something to sell. A monthly LTS cycle plus off-cycle plugin advisories makes the low end unrealistic for a security-sensitive site.

**Jurisdiction can override all of this.** GitHub's trade-controls policy shows the tail risk: restricted organizations lose private repository access and paid Actions minutes, and cannot download or delete that private data without US government authorization, while GHES cannot be sold or exported to Country Group E:1 ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/other-site-policies/github-and-trade-controls)). EU data residency addresses location, not US legal jurisdiction, which is why it does not answer the Dutch or individual objections. Even so, Actions' six-point rise shows no measurable private-sector loss from this concern so far.

The table below applies the evidence as this report's own recommendation, not survey findings.

| Situation | Choose | Condition or first step |
|---|---|---|
| Code already on GitHub, no jurisdiction limit | GitHub Actions | Require lockfiles, use OIDC, and keep a break-glass deploy path for outages |
| Mature Jenkins with scripted pipelines and many plugins | Keep Jenkins, migrate selectively | Run the Importer `audit`, inventory plugins, and shrink the plugin set first |
| Multi-SCM estate or complex release orchestration | Jenkins | Budget real staff time, use ephemeral agents, and treat the sandbox as weak |
| Special hardware or mainframe builds | Actions self-hosted runners, or Jenkins | Bankdata shows Actions can work; test control-plane dependence |
| Small or mid-sized team on a private forge | Gitea Actions | Close registration, patch fast, and mirror actions locally |
| Regulated, needs named deployment approvers | Not Gitea Actions alone today | Add an external CD tool, or wait and reassess after 29.0.0 |
| Strict sovereignty or air gap | Jenkins, Gitea, Forgejo, or GitLab self-managed | Budget for mirrors and patching; compare Forgejo's governance |

## Conclusion

The usual framing of modern YAML against legacy Groovy misses what changed in 2026: all three platforms now carry serious operational risk, and the choice is which risk your team is best placed to absorb. A strong platform team can absorb Jenkins' or Gitea's patching load, while a small one is better served accepting GitHub's outages and hardening its workflows. Whichever you choose, the cheapest insurance is keeping build logic in scripts and containers rather than Groovy or marketplace YAML. Portability is what lets a team respond to a pricing change, a jurisdiction problem, or a bad outage year, and Argo CD's strong recommendation score ([CNCF Radar](https://www.cncf.io/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Q1-2026-CNCF-Technology-Radar-Report.pdf)) suggests separating CI from delivery is a further way to reduce dependence on any one of them, though no source cross-tabulates that pattern.

This assessment has a short shelf life, and four events should trigger a review: enforcement of the `pull_request_target` default on December 2, 2026; Gitea 29.0.0 and whether environment gates follow OIDC; any new date for GitHub's self-hosted fee; and whether GitHub's reliability improves as the database migration completes. The evidence most worth gathering is what nobody has published: measured engineer hours per Jenkins controller, a real person-month figure for a large migration, and a named large-scale Gitea Actions deployment. Until those exist, precise TCO percentages in this debate deserve skepticism.
